Over the past decade, I have moved from fintech, enterprise AI, and data products into consumer software, imaging, and intelligent hardware. The industries look different, but the work I keep returning to is the same: understand what a new technology can solve, find the users and situations worth investing in, and turn that opportunity into a product a team can deliver and a business can validate.

I have worked at Ping An Bank, Baidu, iFLYTEK, ByteDance, and DJI, and I have also started companies. I have built APIs, platforms, and SaaS products as well as consumer AI applications, imaging software, and intelligent hardware. Many of these projects began with little more than a technical opportunity or an ambiguous need, then had to move through research, product definition, team delivery, launch, adoption, and commercial results.

How I build products

I usually begin with three questions:

  • What is the user actually trying to accomplish—not which feature are they asking for?
  • What can the technology do reliably today, and what is still only a demonstration?
  • How should the team be organized so that one validation can become a product that ships repeatedly?

This is why I focus on end-to-end systems. Model quality matters, but it is only one part of a product. Data, tools, interaction, hardware, deployment, operations, and the business model jointly determine what the user ultimately receives.

How the experience connects

In enterprise products, I worked on data platforms, financial risk, business information, OCR/NLP, and intelligent review systems. That work taught me how to turn complex operating problems into reusable product capabilities.

In consumer products and startups, I worked on intelligent editing, AI video, family imaging, and hardware exploration. That work taught me how to begin with a real task and work through experience, growth, and commercialization.

As I moved deeper into imaging and intelligent hardware, more of my attention shifted to the physical world: how software, algorithms, and hardware work as one system; how AI participates in capture, creation, and family life; and how a capability crosses the distance between demo, productization, and delivery.

What I am building now

iRead

iRead is a local-first AI research assistant. A user names the domains they care about, reviews the proposed sources and representative work, then begins collection and receives daily, weekly, and monthly research reports.

I built it because the hardest part of sustained research is often not finding more content. It is continuously separating original evidence from second-hand reporting and duplicated information.

Industry Research Skill

I am developing a research Skill that runs from public-data collection to industry judgment. It connects collection, cleaning, structuring, company comparison, and report generation while preserving sources, conflicts, and uncertainty so conclusions remain auditable.

Personal Knowledge Base

I am also migrating years of industry notes from Feishu into a public knowledge base. The documents will not be copied wholesale. Each one is first reviewed for freshness, privacy, overlap, and long-term maintenance value.

How I research and write

I usually build an industry map first, then move into companies, products, and technology. In writing, I try to separate facts, inference, and position, while preserving the process by which new evidence changes an earlier judgment.

This site is not optimized for daily posting. It is a public workbench: a record of what I have studied, what I am testing now, which views have changed, and how the tools I build support the next research cycle.
