In September 2026, Salesforce completed its acquisition of Fin for approximately $3.6 billion.

Fin is a customer-service AI company with $400 million in annualized revenue, about $100 million of which comes from charging per resolution: every resolved customer conversation generates a fee.

What is the acquisition’s most valuable asset?

My answer: the right to define “resolution.”

## “Resolution” is a contract hidden on the pricing page

Fin’s public rules turn “resolution” into a checklist rather than a word. Which replies count? Does a handoff count? Is a sales lead a different outcome? Which events trigger deductions? Each line is a contract term.

Zendesk goes further with “Verified Resolution.” Who verifies it? Zendesk’s own model.

The problem is visible: the supplier defines the pricing unit, decides whether it was achieved, and issues the invoice.

A third kind of business has emerged in response. TareCount reviews AI support invoices for buyers, separating the vendor’s rules from stricter independent standards. In cloud computing, reconciliation checked API call counts. Now it checks whether this interaction actually counts as resolved.

Further downstream sit the ledgers. Lago remembers what was agreed when prices change frequently. Metronome makes real-time consumption visible as part of procurement trust. The more dynamic pricing becomes, the more invoice explanation becomes a product of its own.

## Electricity meters already performed this play

Every generation meets the confusion of a new pricing unit.

In the 1880s, as electricity entered factories, Edison charged per lamp. Consumption and payment did not match, users objected, and meters arrived: electrolytic, then induction, then mandatory verification. Anyone could generate electricity, but something beyond buyer and seller had to define a kilowatt-hour.

The telephone repeated the story. Per-minute charging created itemized bills; bills created reconciliation systems; reconciliation created independent settlement providers.

The pattern is neat: **a new billing unit moves through free definition, dispute, independent measurement, and standardization.** Whoever controls the definition in the fourth stage collects the next generation’s metering tax.

AI is somewhere between stages one and two. I previously audited six real public usage claims. Every number was self-reported, and none defined its unit. What is “a payment” or “a request”? None of the six said. At a scale of 160 million transactions priced at $0.25, unit ambiguity becomes a financial-integrity issue. Agents add another layer: does a retry count as completion? Does a cache hit count as a call? Even “number of events” is losing a stable meaning.

## Where my flag is

My judgment: **the outcome is moving from pricing language to an object of measurement, and buyers will force independent metering into existence.** Buyer and seller definitions will diverge. When that divergence reaches the financial statements, independent metering appears. Electricity meters were not driven by the power company’s enthusiasm; overcharged factories demanded them.

This is also where AgentMeasure places its bet. Evidence has levels. Missing evidence becomes UNPROVABLE rather than a quiet zero. Its first public case produced an honest 0%. A measurement business should not advocate for either side. It should produce something both sides are willing to sign.

The failure condition: if platforms—model providers plus settlement layers—use their market power to freeze one outcome standard, independent measurement shrinks into a narrow audit business. So I watch one indicator: when large buyers begin demanding third-party reviews of agent invoices. TareCount’s order book measures how wide that door is open.
