## Start with a user who is easy to explain

After several revisions, a sports-video product concept became much clearer.

Its priority user was now “a player who recently took a lesson and has a specific practice assignment.” The choice was easy to explain: the coach assigns a task, the player records the movement, and the coach reviews it. Who uses the product, what they record, and who watches the footage all seemed settled.

The scenario itself was not the problem. The problem was that this easy-to-explain, easy-to-test scenario gradually replaced the broader product question. Recording an activity, sharing it with friends, and making something expressive were pushed into the background.

The concept flowed so well that it became easy to stop asking: what evidence supports this trade-off? The revision notes offered a different reason: it made “what to record and who to show it to” easier to define. That is a sensible research starting point. It is not evidence for excluding everyone else.

## “Record it” can lead to different outcomes

A later version separated training, memory-keeping, and expression again. Training footage needs to help users inspect a movement. A record should preserve a meaningful experience. Expression also requires selection, editing, packaging, and a decision about the audience.

For coach feedback, exporting a file may complete the key delivery. For an activity record, the user may want to return to moments from that day. For expression, a pile of raw footage is not yet something worth sharing. Calling all of this “recording” makes the needs look similar. Follow what happens after the recording, and the differences appear.

This is why “one starting point for the first product” and “one motivation for the research” are different choices. A first version can choose the most confident delivery, provided we know what was removed, why it was deferred, and what evidence would change that decision.

## Engineering simplification changes the problem in three places

First, the user threshold. Moving from “people who actually record their sport” to “people with an assignment from a coach” changes the research population. Second, the output. Moving from watchable, expressive content to a file export changes the completion standard. Third, the scenario. A fixed practice station is easier to support, but it can make a social activity on the move look like an optional use case for later.

Each adjustment may be reasonable on its own. Together, they can turn one product into another. Every narrowing therefore needs an explicit reason: existing evidence, a resource constraint, or a desire to make the current document easier to write.

The third reason is not always wrong. An early concept needs to become concrete before it can be discussed. But it should remain a working hypothesis, not quietly turn into a validated market choice as the document gets polished.

## Research data favors the easy-to-explain scenario too

The process material recorded another limitation: requests for technique feedback were common in the recording discussions sampled from a selected golf community. Community selection matters. A place organized around swing discussion is naturally more likely to contain videos posted for advice.

I did not recalculate that historical analysis for this essay, and it does not represent every golfer’s motivation for sharing. Its real warning is simpler: if you enter through training, the material you collect may keep making the training route look best supported.

Comparing memory-keeping, expression, and training requires material from their respective contexts and observation of existing behavior. Who sets up a phone unprompted? Who never watches the footage? Who spends time editing a clip for a friend? Who only needs to send it to a coach? These differences are more useful than one broad “sports user” label.

## Restoring a direction does not prove its market

A later version restored the compressed goals of memory-keeping, expression, and lightweight fun. It also kept the constraints: distribution, continued use, and IP revenue each needed separate validation. Restoring them made the research question whole again. It did not declare every one of them a business.

This step is easy to write badly. Correcting an over-narrowed concept by treating the original broad vision as entirely correct merely swaps one bias for another. A useful version history preserves both the original intent and the later trade-offs, including the parts that still lack evidence.

The product owner still has to choose. Good process material makes that choice face real alternatives rather than a polished document that has already removed them.

## Keep the conditions that would overturn the choice

The next round can be organized around three outcomes: does the training video improve the next practice session, is the activity record watched again, and is the finished clip shared voluntarily? All three can happen to the same person. There is no need to force three mutually exclusive user segments.

Validation does not require three hardware products either. Start with one body of source footage, compare the different outputs, and only then decide what to automate. That is when engineering investment can finally meet behavioral evidence.

I would leave one slightly inelegant sentence in the product document: “This is the starting point we chose to get moving. We do not yet have evidence that everyone else needs nothing.”

It does not sound like a conclusion. Sometimes the most useful sentence in a document is the one that refuses to impersonate one.

## Case background

This essay compares two process versions and the revision notes of a sports-video concept from my personal research material. Project identity, budget, people, and internal execution details are omitted. It describes how the reasoning changed; it is not a new set of consumer interviews, and it does not claim market validation for the later version. Training, memory-keeping, and expression organize the research questions, not an estimate of three market sizes.
