On September 6, 2026, Beni’s Kickstarter campaign ended with $4,584,657 from 7,293 backers: 9,169% of its goal.

An automatic camera robot had raised more than forty times its target. Headlines dutifully called it a new record.

I have studied this category for a year. When I saw that number, I thought of another list first—the names that fell before this boom.

## The chronology

**2022: the first cooling.** Snap discontinued its Pixy selfie drone months after launch; Skydio later left the consumer market. Large companies paid real money to test a proposition: consumer autonomous filming can sustain a story more easily than a business.

**February 2025: Roll Cam AI.** Calling itself the “world’s first robotic AI cameraman,” it offered a wheeled tripod at a $249–259 VIP presale price, then launched on Kickstarter: roughly $138,000 from 440 backers. In this round of research, I found no subsequent delivery evidence, and its website led to a blank page. Trade coverage was not kind.

**March 12, 2025: Sporttotal.** The German company gave cameras to clubs and monetized the platform. It announced self-administered insolvency proceedings with the goal of restructuring. Shareholders were not the only people exposed: 135 clubs in Bavaria were left waiting.

**2025: 10-Chairs.** The French intelligent stabilized monopod reached 17% of its €80,000 crowdfunding target. There was no continuation.

**September 15, 2026: MOVE'N SEE.** The French company behind the PIXEM tracking camera was ordered into judicial liquidation, according to public records still awaiting final confirmation through court or registry announcements. A veteran of roughly a decade did not survive this cycle.

The survivors changed their lives too. Snoppa’s three-wheeled Rover camera vehicle sells for $2,399 to professional crews. Edelkrone’s robotic camera-movement kit sells for $5,579 to professional creators. XbotGo uses fixed dual cameras for youth sports and entered more than a thousand Best Buy stores. Looper skipped the robot and sells a 3D camera module.

## Three deaths, one cause

Read the chronology vertically, and the failed companies share three traits.

**Consumer pricing multiplied by engineering costs.** A machine that moves, avoids obstacles, and tracks people carries engineering-grade component and reliability costs. The buyer is a consumer giving it a try. Crowdfunding can cover that gap once.

**The illusion of the crowdfunding cash pool.** A 9,169% funding rate proves that people can imagine the category, not that they will keep the product. When the money arrives, the real costs—manufacturing, logistics, support, and returns—start charging interest. It is not simply revenue; it is a liability prepaid by customers.

**Selling movement when users buy footage.** This is the hardest finding in my research: users want a better shot of themselves. Whether the machine moves is the engineer’s excitement. Veo, the company that made the economics work, uses a fixed position and AI-generated tracking. It does not move.

Together: **they recorded applause for a technical demo as evidence of demand.**

The applause was real. It does not pay for the next purchase.

## The list is not finished

I will keep maintaining this chronology: Beni’s delivery, Veo-style products moving into consumer markets, and the spread of camera modules are all on the watchlist.

The criterion is already written down, so I cannot soften it later: the unassisted-session rate—the share of sessions where users never touch the device and still want to publish the result. None of the public material I checked disclosed it. When that number appears in an annual report, this list can turn a page.
