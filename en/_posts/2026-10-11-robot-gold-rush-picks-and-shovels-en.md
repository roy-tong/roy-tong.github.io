In June 2025, a “robotics company” doubled its valuation to $1.25 billion.

Gecko Robotics is best known for a machine that climbs walls with ultrasonic sensors, moving across power-station boilers and naval hulls.

But by the company’s own account, the valuable part is not the climb.

It is what comes after.

## The first lesson of a gold rush: the people selling jeans get rich early

In this embodied-AI boom, the spotlight belongs to companies building bodies: humanoids, quadrupeds, and robotic arms. Funding announcements arrive one after another.

I am more interested in another group. They do not bet on one kind of body. Some do not sell a single robot. Yet they already collect real revenue. I divide them into four layers by their position in the industry.

First, field intelligence. FieldAI announced more than $400 million in cumulative funding in August 2025, including a $314 million round co-led by Bezos Expeditions and Khosla, at a valuation of about $2 billion. Its “Field Foundation Models” provide autonomous capabilities for mobile robots without tying them to one machine. The bet is clear: bodies will commoditize; the intelligence that adapts to different field environments will not.

Second, observability. Rerun and Foxglove build robotics logging and visualization. Rerun’s open-source SDK handles multi-rate, multimodal data streams. Foxglove organizes recording, upload, device indexing, and remote debugging around the open MCAP format. Autonomous driving already proved the pattern: vehicles are debugged through replay, and robots are no different.

Third, inspection data. This is Gecko. In March 2026, it signed a $71 million contract with the US Navy to put wall-climbing robots on ships. Climbing is the collection entrance. Data flows into the Cantilever platform and becomes a living model of the asset. The buyer is no longer purchasing one inspection; it is purchasing maintenance decisions.

Fourth, field coordination. Dusty Robotics does one thing: print digital building models onto construction-site floors. That may not sound like a business. But layout is the shared reference for every trade. The robot’s line lets electrical, plumbing, HVAC, and structural teams work from the same version of the design. It sells “everyone looking at the same drawing.”

## Three mechanisms moving money beyond the body

**First, the most expensive part of robotics is not manufacturing. It is making the thing work.**

Between a prototype and a useful field machine lies a long adaptation process: sensor alignment, scenario tuning, and failure replay. There is no glamorous story here, only tools. Tools mean subscriptions. Rerun and Foxglove occupy that layer: every new machine you debug makes their workflow harder to leave.

**Second, data compounds outside the body.**

Gecko can charge for one wall inspection. But two, five, or ten years of data can answer a different question: will this boiler fail next year? That question earns an annual fee. The body is consumable; data compounds. A hardware company’s second curve is not a faster machine. It is whether the machine’s data can become a decision.

**Third, the buyer changes.**

An engineer buys a single-machine debugging tool from a tools budget. Fleet data management belongs to a team process and a departmental budget. An asset-decision platform addresses the downtime losses a CFO cares about and enters the capital budget. Move the same technology up one layer, and both deal size and renewal logic change. These four kinds of company are keeping different accounts for the same industry.

## The counterexample: a graveyard of bodies

I previously checked a group of consumer camera robots that failed over five years: Roll Cam’s website went blank after crowdfunding, Sporttotal entered insolvency in March 2025, and MOVE'N SEE faced judicial liquidation in September 2026. The problem is not that “bodies” are the wrong direction. Selling only the body, with movement as the value proposition, cannot support consumer pricing multiplied by engineering costs.

The picks-and-shovels companies avoid that multiplication. Their customers are engineers, fleets, and asset owners. They pay from budgets, not impulse.

## Where my flag is

Every new computing platform replays the same story. PCs made antivirus and compilers valuable early. Cloud computing did the same for Datadog and Snowflake. Autonomous driving did it for simulation and labeling. The newer the platform, the earlier observability, tooling, and data become valuable.

My judgment: **the “Datadog of robotics” will emerge before the “iPhone of robotics.”** Companies are still fighting over body shapes while the picks-and-shovels suppliers collect annual fees.

The failure condition belongs here too. If a body company owns a vertical loop from inspection to data to decision, the independent tooling layer gets squeezed. Gecko is moving in that direction itself: it is both the third layer and a potential integrated winner. That is the signal to watch.
