My way of doing research is slightly odd: I keep more than a dozen directions open, require myself to publish a judgment on each, and reverse it when the evidence changes.

A friend says this is not research. It is digging holes for myself.

That sounds about right. This is the ledger for those holes: twelve directions I am tracking in 2026, with three lines for each—the current situation, where I put my flag, and what I will test next.

The rules first. I use only two kinds of material: published research you can open and check, and research discussions explicitly labeled as such. Private budgets, customers, and schedules stay out. Confidence comes in tiers. Claims with financial data sit in one; discussion frameworks sit in another. Do not weigh them equally.

## Twelve directions

**1. Agent measurement.** Flag: every usage number is self-reported. I audited six, with no exception. Test: replicate the “discovery → evidence → fix” loop across another group of observability projects. If it finds no real bug, call the first result luck.

**2. Outcome-based pricing.** Flag: the pricing unit is becoming an object of measurement, and buyers will force independent metering into existence. Test: watch when large buyers begin requiring third-party reviews of agent invoices.

**3. Camera robots.** Flag: movement is both the selling point and an unpaid debt; the player with the best economics does not move. Test: compare fixed and moving camera positions in the same scenario. Measure which result users want to publish, not tracking accuracy.

**4. Sports recording.** Flag: the strongest demand signal is the workaround—such as tying a phone to a chair—not “I want AI.” Test: willingness to pay. Find who will pay to remove the existing friction without adding another feature.

**5. Edge AI.** Flag: a dedicated box must first justify the extra power outlet. Test: compare complete task cost, including setup, waiting, and rework—not benchmarks.

**6. Home robots.** Flag: the central problem is not operation but the cost of failure: the task and its recovery. Test: in real homes, measure who cleans up after the robot fails and how long it takes.

**7. AI glasses.** Flag: keep small product-feedback samples separate from large scenario-expansion samples. Distinguish moments of use, wearing burden, and solution acceptance. Test: abandonment by moment of wear, not questionnaire preference.

**8. AI video creation.** Flag: the moat is the second edit—preserve what was approved and change only what was requested. Test: after one explicit edit request, check whether anything else changed unintentionally.

**9. Brain-computer interfaces and measurable signals.** Flag: questionnaires measure imagination. The product question is which decision changes after a signal becomes measurable. Test: identify one decision that genuinely changes because of that signal.

**10. Demand-research methods.** Flag: the problem, solution acceptance, and commercial behavior require separate proof. Volume cannot substitute for adoption. Test: apply SURE’s evidence levels to the next real project.

**11. Hardware organizations.** Flag: the conflict between software and hardware delivery rhythms shapes the organization. Shared employment history does not prove causation. Test: compare delivery cycles in two teams of similar size.

**12. Research tools themselves.** Flag: tool count cannot substitute for original research count. This one is for me too. Test: ask whether iRead, SURE, and the content pipeline each produce research that would not exist without them.

## Why publish the map?

Three reasons.

First, a public judgment creates an obligation. Once I say movement is debt, I have to return when Beni ships. I cannot quietly forget.

Second, it helps me be wrong faster. A visible flag lets other people point out the wrong one. That is the cheapest source of counterevidence.

Third, these directions look unrelated but repeat one pattern: a discovered problem becomes presumed adoption, a capability becomes presumed delivery, and a log becomes presumed value. Every direction is stuck at one of those gates.

Stopping at a judgment is safe. Stopping at a validation list is honest. I choose the latter.

**Boundaries and sources:** This is the direction view of my 2026 research review. The original judgments and evidence appear there. Every validation item has a failure condition. These directions are not investment advice, and the flags will move with the evidence.
